Yesterday,Travis Davisreplied to the email that went out with my post about Michael Lewis’Home Game. He’s a Lewis fan, a father of two boys (6 and 3), and he had just written a book titledRanch Kid to Rocket Scientist: The Field Manual.

He described the people he grew up around in rural South Dakota this way:

“In reality they were doing the work, but their style was build, test repeat and less analysis paralysis / building paper machines that would never meet hardware.”

I ordered the book after I finished reading the email and then started a back and forth with Travis about it.

Travis wroteRanch Kid to Rocket Scientist: The Field Manualwith his wife,Amanda Davis. The premise is simple. A kid who fixes fence, hauls hay, runs irrigation lines, and pulls engines is already doing engineering, but nobody tells them that. The tractor’s hydraulics are a rocket engine’s control valves, the air compressor is a pressure vessel, welding and running a cutting torch are metallurgy, and the trusses in a pole barn are structural engineering.

Each chapter pairs a story from Travis’s childhood with outdoor experiments in structural mechanics, fluid dynamics, thermodynamics, propulsion, orbital mechanics, atmospheric science, and engineering design. The materials come from the ranch or the hardware store, so no lab is required.

“We figured it out eventually, but it took leaving the ranch to learn what the ranch had already taught us.”

Most leadership content is written by people who have never fixed a fence. I think the leadership that matters for the next generation comes from people who did the hard, prestigious thing and then turned around to show the kids who are where they once were how to get there. Between them, Travis and Amanda tested rocket engines at Blue Origin and controlled payload operations for the International Space Station, and then they wrote a field manual for ranch kids. Travis said why in his email:

“If I can help 1 kid overcome … the imposter syndrome they might feel at engineering school, NASA or whatever their dream might be, then this would be well worth my time.”

I expect he and Amanda will help a lot more than one kid.

Travis grew up on a cattle ranch in northwestern South Dakota, 40 miles from the nearest hardware store. He got a mechanical engineering degree from South Dakota Mines, interning at NASA four times along the way, and then went to Trinity College Dublin as a Mitchell Scholar. At Blue Origin, he led the engine test team at Test Stand 4670, the same stand that tested Saturn V engines. Now he’s at Fermilab working on theDeep Underground Neutrino Experiment, whose far detector is being built nearly a mile underground in Lead, South Dakota, in the Black Hills. That’s where he and Amanda live now with their boys.

I’ve spent time in the Black Hills twice. I ranmarathon #25 at Crazy Horsein 2017 (a personal worst at the time), and on a separate trip I took my dad and my brother there, where we hung out, visited dinosaur stuff, had some Dairy Queen Blizzards, and went to Mount Rushmore.

Amanda was a NASA engineer and a certified Payload Operations Controller for the International Space Station. She foundedBioFitand, per her website, “Traded Space Station Telemetry for Glucose Telemetry,” now teaching professionals how to read the data coming off glucose monitors and other wearables.

MIT recently launchedMIT for Americato bring STEM education, with an emphasis on math and hands-on making, to learners from kindergarten through community college in all 50 states, South Dakota included. Inher announcement, President Sally Kornbluth described it as “grounded in our deep belief in potential over pedigree,” and she tied it to learning by doing. That sounds pretty similar to what Travis is telling ranch kids.

Learning by doing is Travis’s “build, test repeat,” and it’s whatSphero, a company I’m on the board of, has spent years putting in the hands of kids and teachers withprogrammable robots. A ranch kid gets that experience from a tractor, and a kid in a classroom can get it from a robot. In my email with Travis, he mentioned that Amanda had already decided their boys were getting Sphero for Christmas, so I introduced him to Paul Copioli, Sphero’s CEO.

If you have a kid, a grandkid, or a classroom full of kids who work hard and think big,order a copy.

Travis and Amanda - congrats on the book.
