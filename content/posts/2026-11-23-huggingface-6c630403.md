# How to build and maintain an agentic evaluation

## Overview

AI evaluations help shape which systems people build, adopt, and trust. An evaluation gives a system tasks, checks its answers or actions, and produces a score. Building and maintaining a useful evaluation therefore takes real work: defining tasks that reflect domain expertise, scoring them reliably, and checking that the results support the claims made. When methods, code, and data stay private, that work is harder for others to scrutinize or build on.

Open source offers a means to build stronger, more frequently updated, and more comparable evaluations and the infrastructure around them.  Existing tasks, environments, and tools give builders a starting point, so their effort can go to domain expertise and validation. Sharing how an evaluation was built, including what went wrong, lets others find errors, improve it, and reuse it.

The Hugging Face hub is the home for AI evaluation: we host around 3500 eval leaderboards and have seen about a16X growth in eval datasets on the hub in the past year, to about 17000 datasets. In recent months, we have also incorporated support for agentic abstractions that are relevant to evaluation. For example,Tracesthat store an agent’s work in an easily reviewable and sharable way;Benchmarksthat highlight datasets used to evaluate models with leaderboards; andEnvironmentsthat show datasets used as agentic RL or evaluation environments.

This guide covers four things:

- Three eras of evaluation(section 1). Evaluations moved from checking answers, to checking what an agent did, to checking what a team of agents produces. Each step adds software that can change the score.
- The steps in an agentic evaluation(section 2). We follow one task through eight steps, from the claim to the reported score. The full walkthrough, with commands, is inAppendix A.
- When a benchmark is wrong(section 3). Errors in tasks and graders are common. We look at how they are found and corrected.
- What to open strategically and why(section 4). A table sets out, component by component, what to share and what can stay protected.

In the research community, AI evaluation terminology isnotoriously inconsistent. Theglossaryat the end defines each term we use and notes the other names you may come across.

## 1. Introduction: how evaluation design has changed

AI evaluation design has evolved in three forms as language models have become more capable and able to perform more complex work: question answer, agentic, and teams or swarms. Question-answer benchmarks such asMMLU(2020) andGPQA(2023) measure knowledge and reasoning through the answers models produce. As models became capable of planning and using tools, agentic benchmarks such asSWE-benchandWebArenaexpanded the scope to resolving software issues and completing tasks on websites. These tasks require evaluation setups that account for the environment in which the system operates and the changes it makes.

Lately, research on multi-agent systems has started to add another dimension: how agents coordinate and what they accomplish together. This includesdecentralized swarms,teams managed by an orchestrator, andspecialized agents collaborating on scientific work. Figure 1 shows a rough timeline of these three eras of evaluations.

Figure 1. Three types of language model evals. Question-and-answer benchmarks check a response against a key. Agentic benchmarks check what an agent changed in an environment. Team and swarm benchmarks score what a group of agents produced.

We’ll focus on agentic evaluations in this article for two reasons. First, they are becoming increasingly the most prominent type inmodel release announcementsdue to high user and enterprise interest in agentic deployment. Second, they offer a useful starting point for understanding how evaluation design affects model performance. Team and swarm evaluations introduce further complexity through interactions between agents, which we leave outside the scope of this guide. Once an agent uses tools to complete a task, each part of the surrounding software can affect its score. For instance, in anaudit of Terminal-Bench, researchers kept the model fixed and changed the agent harness. Accuracy varied by up to 29.9 percentage points. To understand differences like that, we examine the anatomy of an agentic evaluation and follow one task from the instructions the agent receives to the checks that determine whether it passed.

### The anatomy of an agentic evaluation

Figure 2. The different components of an agentic evaluation.

There are various parts to an agentic evaluation. A task gives the agent its instruction. The agent harness wraps the model and its tools, letting it work inside a pinned environment. During one rollout, the agent attempts the task from start to finish: it may run several commands, inspect their output, and try again before it stops. The evaluation harness runs several rollouts for each task. When a rollout ends, the verifier grades the environment's final state. Each rollout also leaves a trace, and traces and scores together become part of the final published evidence. Each part can be published, inspected, and reused separately.

### Why benchmark release decisions matter

Demand for independent evaluation is growing. Policymakers, enterprises, and researchers want evaluations they can verify, and today they mostly cannot:98.2% of model-benchmark scores are reported by only one party, and researchers have argued thatin-house evaluation is not enough.

At the same time, more benchmarks are holding back test items to reduce contamination: questions or solutions entering training data, so scores may reflect prior exposure. Artificial Analysissays 60% of its Intelligence Index v5, announced in October 2026, rests on evaluations with private test sets or held-out answers. Google DeepMind recently piloteddouble-blind evaluations, where a secure enclave hides an outside evaluator's test prompts from the model provider and the model's weights from the evaluator. While that can reduce contamination, privacy by itself does not guarantee quality, and withholding supporting materials can make expert review harder. We argue that builders can and should still share infrastructure, grading methods, and evidence about task design. Scrutiny depends on giving others a way to question the measurement and correct mistakes, including controlled review where public release is unsuitable.

## 2. Building an eval: what to reuse, adapt, and validate

With the anatomy in place, we now build one agentic evaluation for a real use case: an agent that analyzes measurements for a physics lab. Rather than starting from scratch, we adapt an open task, so most of our effort goes into domain expertise and validation.

We work through eight steps:define the claim,find a task,run it unchanged,adapt it,check the evaluation,read the traces,report the score, andcompare models. We then take stock of what we reused. The full walkthrough is inAppendix A. Figure 3 maps the steps and what each produced in our example.

### Method

Claim.Consider a physics lab that wants an agent to fit peaks in a Raman spectrum. A Raman spectrum is a measurement whose peaks reveal properties of a material, here graphene. The agent's job is ordinary coding: read the data file, fit a curve to each peak, and save the results (Figure 5 in Appendix A). For the purposes of this article, our claim is deliberately narrow: fit the specified peaks, calculate the requested D-to-G ratio (the intensity of one peak relative to another, which can indicate defects in the material; we also call it the defect ratio), and save accurate results consistently. Reviewing a whole lab report would need additional skills and evidence.

Starting task and baseline.We reused theHarbortask format and Terminal-Bench'sraman-fittingtask. Harbor is an open source evaluation harness, and Terminal-Bench is an open benchmark of tasks that an agent completes in a terminal. We first ran the task's reference solution, which passed, and then ran the original task three times withTerminus-2, a terminal agent harness, andgpt-oss-120b, an open-weight model from OpenAI. Harbor ran inside an HF Job, each rollout used its own HF Sandbox, and Inference Providers supplied the model responses. Reusing the runner and task format let us concentrate on the physics, grading, and review. Only the agent harness needed no new work. Every other component needed checking or new work, which we list part by part atthe end of Appendix A.

Adaptation.We builtraman-lab: eight synthetic spectra with known parameters and varied noise and defect levels. The adaptation fits three peaks instead of two and reports a defect ratio. We used an agent to help generate the spectra, reviewed them by hand, and wrote new instructions, reference fits, and output checks. The new instructions state the units and parameter definitions that the original left implicit. These eight tasks are a development set, meaning tasks we use while building the evaluation; broader coverage should follow the variation in real measurements.

Checks.Before executing more model runs, we tested the evaluation itself. We ran the reference solution, an agent that does nothing, and a shortcut that skips the required work. The reference solution should pass every task, and the other two should fail. Our shortcut read the height of each peak straight from the data and guessed the widths. It returned the expected defect ratio on all eight tasks without fitting the spectra, so we added checks on the fit itself.

Shortcut.We also tested a shortcut to check how robust the evaluation is against cheating. Our shortcut is a short script that skips the actual fitting. It reads the tallest point of each peak straight from the data, guesses the peak widths, and divides the D height by the G height. Even so, it returned the expected defect ratio on all eight tasks, so a grader that checked only the ratio would have given it full marks. We added checks on each fitted peak, and the final verifier rejects it.

## 3. Bitter lessons: when a benchmark is wrong

Our shortcut returned the expected defect ratio without fitting a spectrum. A verifier that checked only the ratio would have given it full marks on all eight tasks. We found it by trying to break the grader with a solution we knew was incomplete, then added checks on the fitted spectrum (Appendix A, Step 5).

The shortcut got the right answer for the wrong reason, like a student who copies the final number without the working. The lesson for us was to test what the output demonstrates. A faster valid method should pass; a method that skips a required skill should not. Domain experts can help set tolerances so the verifier accepts alternative correct fits without accepting work that misses the point of the task.

Such errors are common, and they move scores considerably. Areview of agentic benchmarksfound that flaws in task setup or reward design can shift measured performance by up to 100% in relative terms.

### Public review is essential for correcting benchmarks

Public review can expose errors in instructions and tests that benchmark builders missed. Terminal-Bench 2.1 fixed28 of its 89 tasksafter user reports, including one whose tests expected Spark SQL while its instructions asked for PostgreSQL, andagent rankings stayed stablethrough the correction.

Access to the evidence also determines how much work an outside audit takes. CritPt keeps its reference solutions private, so thephysicists who audited ithad to solve the problems themselves before finding errors in 21 of the 56 challenges they reviewed. The authors then released acorrected version. OpenAI's audit ofSWE-bench Verifiedfound material issues in 59.4% of the 138 problems it examined, working from tasks and tests that anyone can read. For a builder, outside scrutiny is part of validation: publish what reviewers need to challenge a task, give them a way to report problems, and record what changed.

Similar flaws emerged in SWE-bench Pro. In July 2026 OpenAIestimated that about 30% of SWE-Bench Pro tasks were brokenand withdrew its recommendation to adopt it. Benchmark validation needs to continue after release.

### Check the claim as well as the code

A consistent score can still measure the wrong thing.Measuring what Matters, a review of 445 LLM benchmarks by 29 expert reviewers, found recurring patterns in tasks and metrics that undermine construct validity: whether the tasks and metrics support claims about the intended capability.

For example, fitting synthetic spectra does not establish that an agent can review lab reports. That may also require recognizing a poor measurement or asking for help. If every task supplies a usable spectrum, the test cannot show whether the agent knows when a fit would be inappropriate.

Harder Than the Taskextends this concern to organizational work, where authority, distributed knowledge, and dependencies can decide success. Its authors find that fewer than one quarter of prominent benchmarks claim to cover any such dimension. An agent may need calibration data from another team or permission to replace a measurement, and supplying everything at the outset removes those difficulties. If they matter to the claim, they belong in the evaluation. If they do not, it is enough to say that they remain untested.

Each error is worth a short record: the failed assumption, how it was found, and what changed. The record should also name the affected versions and say whether earlier scores remain comparable.

### Contamination vs saturation

The SWE-bench Verified audit described above also found contamination: every frontier model tested could reproduce the reference patch or verbatim problem details for some tasks. SWE-bench's public repositories and wide use exposed tasks to training. The openness that enabled scrutiny of bad tests also made items easier to encounter before evaluation.

However, if contamination is carefully controlled for during training, hiding the test set by itself does not prevent saturation. Astudy of 60 language model benchmarksfound similar saturation in public and private ones, and concluded that hiding test data does not prevent saturation once a benchmark is widely adopted, meaning a good benchmark falls out of use due to saturation without developers being able to learn from and build on it. What did correspond to lower saturation was expert curation and a larger test set.

This leaves a practical release question: what can we expose for review while protecting active test items?

## 4. Open sourcing an evaluation strategically

Balancing contamination risks against saturation requires a strategic approach to what content is made public. Rather than treating openness as an all-or-nothing decision, benchmark creators can decide what to release part by part, protecting active test tasks from data leakage while still sharing the underlying infrastructure, grading methods, and reference examples that allow external researchers to audit and build upon the evaluation.

Existing benchmarks show a range of openness.ARC-AGI-2publishes a training set and a 120-task evaluation set, and holds back semi-private and private sets of 120 tasks each.AILuminatekeeps its official prompts confidential, publishes its methods and taxonomy, and keeps the underlying evidence for controlled review by authorized auditors and trusted researchers. Terminal-Bench publishes its tasks and marks their files with acanary stringasking that they stay out of training corpora. No public reviewer can check a protected item, so whatever stays closed needs a review route of its own.

Our own example follows this approach. We released raman-lab as adataset on the Hub. It includes eight synthetic graphene spectra in Harbor task format, each with its environment, reference fit, verifier, and tolerances. The raw rollouts behind our results are shared asa separate datasetincluding Harbor trial folders with trajectories and verifier output for all six models. The spectrum generator, analysis code, and results are in a companionGitHub repository, so both the agent harness and the evaluation harness can be inspected and rerun.

In a more sensitive domain, a clinical evaluation could open its environment definition, harness, tool interfaces, and grading framework, with synthetic cases for development use. Patient records and reference answers would stay under controlled access while authorized reviewers inspect disputed judgments. Each restriction should come with a condition for revisiting it.

### Strategic open sourcing checklist

We recommend a short checklist, drawn in part from the more comprehensiveAgentic Benchmark Checklist. Before releasing or retiring an evaluation, check that another builder can:

- Identify what it measures, its intended use, and the conditions it leaves out.
- Find the reusable artifacts, their licenses and versions, and a way to report problems.
- See how a do-nothing agent scores and, where possible, how a human expert does.
- Read each score with its uncertainty, and with guidance on what known flaws mean for it.
- Learn which flaws were found, how they were handled, and how far they could move the results.
- Know how and when tasks will be refreshed to limit contamination and overfitting.

These release decisions also determine what remains useful when the benchmark no longer separates the systems it was built to compare.

## 5. Conclusion: giving the evaluation a useful afterlife

Benchmarks have a lifecycle. They are built, adopted, optimized against, and in time stop separating the systems they were made to compare. Thesaturation studyfrom section 3 rated 29 of its 60 language model benchmarks as highly saturated, with rates rising with age. Saturation is not a failure in itself, since it can mean the capability has arrived.

What a benchmark leaves behind depends on how open it was. Publishing only scores leaves future builders little they can run or adapt. When the tasks, environments, verifiers, and traces are open, each keeps working after the leaderboard stops. A solved task becomes a regression check for a new harness. Its environment and verifier can seed a harder successor. Open benchmarks also get repaired and reissued: MMLU's questions becameMMLU-Redux, and Terminal-Bench 2.0 became2.1through community contributions. Our own walkthrough is an afterlife of this kind: raman-lab began as one task in a benchmark built for general terminal work.

That is the promise of building evaluations openly. The work that goes into an evaluation can outlive the benchmark itself, giving others a foundation to ask new questions, test new capabilities, and improve on what came before. The more we share what we build and learn, the less each new evaluation has to start from scratch.

If you’re curious what this looks like in practice, then follow our practical example.

## Appendix A. Practical example: agentic deployment in a physics lab

Consider a physics lab that wants an agent to fit peaks in a Raman spectrum. Raman spectroscopy shines a laser on a sample and measures shifts in the frequency of the scattered light; the resulting peaks reveal properties of the material. For graphene, an atom-thick sheet of carbon, the G and 2D peaks near 1580 and 2700 cm⁻¹identify the number of layers. A third peak, D, appears when the lattice is disordered, and its intensity relative to Gcan indicate defects. Figure 5 shows the setup.

### Step 1. Define the claim

A good place to start is aclaim: a plain statement of what the agent can do, which the evaluation is meant to support. "The agent can fit a Raman spectrum" is a claim, and so is "the agent can process a lab report." From there, you can break the claim into smaller skills that can be checked one at a time, such as reading the file, locating the peaks, and writing the output.

For the purposes of this article, our claim is deliberately narrow: fit the specified peaks, calculate the requested D-to-G ratio, and save accurate results consistently. Reviewing a whole lab report would need additional skills and evidence.

Domain experts can help here. We suggest asking them to choose realistic noise levels, tolerances, and available software, then score examples independently and investigate disagreements. From there, you can break the claim into parts you can check and map each part to what each task tests.

### Step 2. Find a starting task

For your own domain, the Hugging Face Hub is a good place to look, under its Benchmark and Environment dataset types. When you find a candidate, it is worth inspecting its instructions, environment,reference solution, and tests. Even a task that models already solve can provide useful infrastructure. From raman-fitting we inherit a working task format. It has only one spectrum, though, and a lab's measurements vary, so we expand on this task further as described in Step 4.

### Step 3. Run the task unchanged

Before running any model, it helps to run the reference solution (also called oracle). If it fails, the fault lies in the task, environment, or verifier, and needs to be fixed before you read anything into model failures. After that, you can run the model several times without changing the setup.

We ran Harbor inside anHF Job, which supplied compute for the runner. Each rollout used its ownHF Sandboxfor commands and files, andInference Providerssupplied the model responses. With Harbor'shf-sandboxextra installed andHF_TOKENset, these commands test the oracle, then run three rollouts withTerminus-2, a terminal agent harness, andgpt-oss-120b, an open-weight model from OpenAI.

```bash
harbor run -t terminal-bench/raman-fitting \
  -a oracle -e hf-sandbox

OPENAI_API_KEY="$HF_TOKEN" harbor run \
  -t terminal-bench/raman-fitting -k 3 -e hf-sandbox \
  -a terminus-2 -m openai/openai/gpt-oss-120b \
  --ak api_base=https://router.huggingface.co/v1

```

In these commands,-tnames the task,-athe agent,-ethe environment,-mthe model, and-kthe number of rollouts per task.

### Step 4. Adapt the task

We builtraman-lab: eight synthetic spectra with known parameters and varied noise and defect levels. The adaptation fits three peaks instead of two and reports a defect ratio. We used an agent to help generate the spectra, reviewed them by hand, and wrote new instructions, reference fits, and output checks. These eight tasks are a development set, meaning tasks we use while building the evaluation; broader coverage should follow the variation in real measurements.

The D-to-G intensity ratio can indicate defects, but its interpretationdepends on the laser excitation energy and the disorder regime. An evaluation asking for a "defect ratio" must say which peaks, whether intensity means height or integrated area, and how the result is checked.

The originalinstruction.mdshows why such definitions matter: it asks for x0, gamma, amplitude, and offset without defining them or their units. The reference solution treats them as peak center, half-width at half-maximum, height above background, and background level, and converts the x-axis from nanometres to Raman shift. We put choices like these into the adapted instructions, so that the verifier's requirements are visible. Figure 6 shows the two files side by side.

Figure 6. Theinstruction.mdfiles forraman-fittingandraman-lab, side by side. The highlighted additions cover the new parts of the claim: the D peak, the defect ratio, and both in the saved results. The remaining part, handling varied measurements, lives in the data: all eight raman-lab tasks share this instruction, and only the spectrum, with its noise and defect level, changes.

The verifier is guided by three rules:

- Grade what the agent produced: inspect the saved output, not its claim to have finished.τ-benchdoes this by comparing the final database state with an annotated goal state.
- State every checked fact in the instruction, including units, parameter conventions, tolerances, and required fields.
- Generate synthetic data from known parameters so the reference answer is available, then review whether the examples represent the intended problem.

The shared format can stay as it is while the data and checks change. This command creates a task scaffold:

```bash
harbor task init my-org/task-01 -p datasets/my-evals

```

### Step 5. Check the evaluation

Before executing more model runs, test the evaluation itself. Heredatasets/my-evalscontains the adapted tasks.

```bash
harbor run -p datasets/my-evals -a oracle -e hf-sandbox
harbor run -p datasets/my-evals -a nop -e hf-sandbox

```

- The oracle should pass every task, i.e., the known solution must work in the supplied environment.
- The no-op agent does nothing, so it should fail every task. A blank exam paper should not pass. Areview of agentic benchmarksfound one that counted empty responses as successes.
- A shortcut that skips required work should fail. One way to test this is with a deliberately lazy solution, such as one that outputs a plausible answer without doing the work, to see whether the verifier accepts it. Also try plausible wrong answers and alternative correct methods, to catch both false passes and false failures.

Our shortcut returned the expected defect ratio on all eight tasks without fitting the spectra, so we added checks on the fit itself. Section 3 explains what that means for verifier design.

It is helpful to read both successful and failed rollouts. Harbor records messages, tool calls, and observations inagent/trajectory.jsonusingATIF, a shared trace format. Grouping failures by cause makes the patterns easier to see: parsing the data, executing a tool, stopping too early, or grading the output.

Preinstalling changes the evaluation condition, so keep the model, harness, tasks, and rollout count fixed when comparing the two setups.

Finally, we adapt the setup, repeat the checks in step 5, and rerun. Harbor'strace exportercan prepare records for sharing on the Hub. It is worth reviewing them for protected content first, and keeping both successes and failures so others can investigate the result.

### Step 7. Report the score

The right metric depends on the decision you need to make:

- pass@kasks whether at least one of k rollouts succeeds. Our pass@3 was 7/8: seven tasks succeeded at least once.
- pass^kasks whether all k rollouts succeed. Our pass^3 was 1/8: one task succeeded every time.

Retries may be acceptable when a person reviews the output. Unattended processing needs consistency, so the second measure matters more.

A mean is more useful with its uncertainty beside it. Rollouts of the same spectrum share its difficulty, so they are not independent, and the standard error should beclustered by task. Clustering gave a mean success rate of 0.46 with a standard error of about 0.11. A repeat run passed 10 of 24 rollouts, compared with 11 of 24 in the first run. With only eight development tasks, we would be cautious about generalizing either result to new spectra.

Harbor writes aresult.jsonper rollout, which it calls a trial. We group these by task, check that each task has three rollouts, and record errors or exclusions. Reporting tokens, elapsed time, and tool calls beside the score lets readers compare setups that reach similar results at different costs.

### Step 8. Compare models

When a single model fails a task, that run alone cannot tell us what went wrong. It could be the model, the agent harness, or the evaluation itself. Running more models on the same tasks helps narrow the possibilities. When several models fail the same task, inspect the instructions, environment, and verifier for a shared cause. When one model repeatedly fails in a distinctive way, compare its traces with successful attempts.

Inference Providers serves many open-weight models through the same router, so each new model is one more value for-m. Several providers can serve the same model with different quantization and context limits, so it helps to pin one with a suffix such as:deepinfraand record it beside the score.

## Glossary

Agent harness:The software around a model that turns its responses into actions, such as shell commands. Terminus-2 is the agent harness in this post.

Agentic evaluation:An evaluation that scores what an agent changed in an environment, not only the text it returned.

ATIF:Agent Trajectory Interchange Format, a shared format for recording an agent's messages, tool calls and observations.

Canary string:A unique marker in benchmark files that lets model trainers find and exclude them from training data.

Clustered standard error:A standard error that treats rollouts of the same task as related, which gives a wider and more honest margin than treating each rollout as independent.

Container image:A fixed package of operating system, files and software, so every rollout starts from the same environment.

Contamination:Test questions or solutions appearing in training data, so a score may reflect memory rather than skill.

Context limit:The maximum amount of text, counted in tokens, that a model can read in one request.

D, G and 2D peaks:Features in graphene's Raman spectrum. G and 2D indicate the number of layers, and D appears when the carbon lattice has defects.

Defect ratio:The intensity of the D peak divided by that of the G peak, used to indicate defects in graphene. Also called the D-to-G ratio.

Development set:Tasks used while building and debugging an evaluation, as opposed to a held-back test set used for final scores.

Environment:The software, files and resources an agent works in during a task.

Evaluation harness:The software that runs tasks, repeats rollouts, collects traces and aggregates scores. Harbor is the evaluation harness in this post.

Gated dataset:A Hugging Face dataset that can be downloaded only after the owner approves an access request.

Harbor:An open-source evaluation harness for agentic tasks.

HF Job:Compute on Hugging Face that runs a script. Here it runs Harbor.

HF Sandbox:An isolated Hugging Face environment where a single rollout runs its commands and stores its files.

Held-out test set:Test items kept private so that models cannot be trained or tuned on them.

Inference Providers:A Hugging Face service that routes model requests to third-party providers through one API, called the router.

Lorentzian:A mathematical curve commonly used to model the shape of a spectral peak.

No-op agent:An agent that does nothing. A sound evaluation gives it a score of zero.

Open-weight model:A model whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download and run.

Oracle:An agent that runs the reference solution, used to check that every task can be solved in the supplied environment.

pass@k:The share of tasks where at least one of k rollouts succeeds.

pass^k:The share of tasks where all k rollouts succeed.

Pinning:Fixing the exact versions of software, images or model providers so a run can be repeated.

Quantization:Storing model weights at lower numerical precision to run faster and cheaper, which can change outputs slightly.

Raman shift:The difference in frequency between the laser light and the light a sample scatters, measured in cm⁻¹.

Raman spectroscopy:A technique that shines a laser on a sample and measures shifts in the scattered light to identify properties of the material.

Reference solution:A known correct way to complete a task, stored with the task.

Rollout:One attempt by an agent at one task. Harbor calls it a trial.
