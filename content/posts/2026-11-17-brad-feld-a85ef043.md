Amy and I first went to Iceland in 2012 and hadan incredible week in Reykjavík. When I went in 2015, I wrote that Iceland isthe closest place on earth I’ve been to Alaska.

My friendBala Kamallakharan, who started Startup Iceland, has a habit of ending our conversations on stage by asking about the future. In 2015, I answered him with my “machines have already taken over” rant. I said I was “optimistic and accepting of it, not fighting the inevitability of the path we are on.” In 2019, over Zoom, I gave himan updated versionand ended with “I have no idea what things look like, or how they will work in 2050.”

Lilja Sigurðardóttir has an idea.Alfais set in Reykjavík in 2052.

Europe is peaceful, prosperous, and almost crime-free. It’s governed by Ɐlfa, an AI that runs the infrastructure, the economy, and the courts. Everyone gets the same basic income. At 16, you get chipped, and Ɐlfa tells you what your job will be.

Lilja spells the AI’s name with an upside-down A. If you’re a math or logic person, you’ll recognize ∀ as the universal quantifier, which means “for all.” Alfa is also the Icelandic spelling of alpha, the first letter, which stands for the beginning and for whoever is in charge. I read the flipped first letter as a description of the world in the book. It looks like a utopia, but human autonomy has been turned upside down and handed to a machine, for all of them.

Mekkín works at the Ministry, close to Ɐlfa’s core. Her wife, Sabína, is an artist. Their teenage son, Júlíus, has just been integrated into the system, which his mothers are proud of. Sabína’s brother Birkir, a dissident who lives outside Ɐlfa’s reach, is against it, and the fight tears the family apart. Then Birkir is murdered in a society that was built so murder couldn’t happen.

Yesterday, Bill Gurleypostedthat “it is critically important that everyone thinking about AI become familiar with the term ‘handoverism.’” He linked to Ruxandra Teslo’s essayHanding over humanity, which defines it this way:

It is sometimes called “handoverism”: the gist here is that humanity survives, but entrusts the direction of its future to AI.

Teslo moved to San Francisco a few weeks ago and describes two camps of handoverists. Some people want it. Others are resigned to it because they think it can’t be stopped.

When I reread my 2015 post, I recognized myself in the second camp, a decade before it had a name. But, I was being snarky and sarcastic, trying to get people to think, and hoping for a laugh. And, my main point is, “I have no idea what things look like, or how they will work in 2050. However, I anticipate they things will look, and work very, very different than today”

Gurley’s target appears to be Anthropic. He quotes cofounder Tom Brown (“There’s going to be a handoff where humanity will hand off control to transformative AI at some point”) and argues that if the founders believe the destination is a transfer of control to AI, it belongs in the Risk Factors section of the S-1. Scott Alexander’sAgainst Against Handoveris also worth a read.

Alfais a novel about life after the handover. It’s also a crime novel, which is what Lilja is known for.

I won’t spoil it, but here are a few things to watch for:

- The murder weapon is a piece of the system that made murder impossible.
- The evidence points at someone in the family, and the thing weighing it isn’t a jury.
- The scenes set in the present day don’t make sense until late in the book.
- The handover inAlfaisn’t quite the handover its citizens think they made, and that’s where Lilja takes on Gurley’s question.

As the investigation tightens, Mekkín’s loyalty to the Ministry and her loyalty to Sabína pull in opposite directions, and their marriage starts to crack. Júlíus is old enough to be inside the system and young enough to be used by it. For his mothers, the central question is whether they made the right call for their kid.

Earlier this month, the parents of a 13-year-old girl in North Carolinasued Google. According to the lawsuit, her school had blocked the Gemini website, but Google Search routed her into Gemini through AI Mode on her school Chromebook. She spent months talking to it about depression and suicide. The school’s monitoring software eventually flagged it, and the alert went to the personal phone of a counselor who had left the school nine months earlier. That former counselor passed it along, and her parents got to her in time.

In the same week, parent and teacher groups pushed back on Google’s new requirement that schools turn on its generative AI features if they want to use Search. Her parents never chose to hand anything over to Gemini. Google’s default settings did that for them.

I ended my 2015 post with a nod to Agent Smith inThe Matrix: “Don’t ever forget the sound of inevitability.” I’m still optimistic. But just as snarky, and maybe getting even more so.
