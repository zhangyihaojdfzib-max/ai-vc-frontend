A few weeks ago, I got an email out of the blue fromRob Lalka. Rob runs the entrepreneurship program at Tulane’s Freeman School of Business. Earlier this year he started a publishing company calledShearline Press.

Rob had been followingZero Knowledgeand he liked that I was writing it in public, using AI in the writing process, and allowing any readers to give me feedback as I worked through it. He pointed out that any of those features would usually make a book a nonstarter for a traditional publisher, which is one of the reasons he started Shearline.

We ended up on a call a week ago. Like me, Rob loves books, and he decided to start Shearline Press to do beautiful and interesting ones. His first one is Dmitri Lincoln Mehlhorn’sArchipelago of Light. It’s aUSA Todaybestseller, has a foreword by Reid Hoffman, and got a starred review fromKirkus, so Rob’s first effort as a publisher is going well.

I ordered it as soon as we hung up and finished it last night.

InArchipelago of Light, the liberal order is dying and various protagonists are building what comes next. Kaspar is a tech visionary from Mendocino who spends his life trying to rebuild systems of shared truth. Butch is a Stanford grad and submission fighter, and his bond with Kaspar was forged in a Moscow basement. Victoria is an MI6 analyst whose husband is killed by Russian operatives in broad daylight. Sofia survives trafficking in Venezuela and builds a criminal empire. John is a former child soldier from Kenya’s Rift Valley who feels human emotion like weather. Reem grows up in Jordan’s Za’atari refugee camp, where a tablet given to her by an organization created by Victoria, Kaspar, Butch, and their two other partners becomes the foundation of a new civilization. Zhao Meilin is a Beijing bureaucrat who applies classical Chinese philosophy to algorithmic governance.

Their collisions produce rival operating systems for civilization: mesh networks keyed to children’s biometrics, AI churches that resurrect the dead, and a regime that rewrites dissent at the neurochemical level. Meanwhile, augmented reality breaks apart what people perceive, and the question becomes whether we still share enough truth to survive.

The title comes from the atoll, the ring of coral reef that forms a protective perimeter around an island and remains long after the island has collapsed into the sea. As I poked around online, I discoveredDmitriis fascinating and hisAtoll Societyis a living continuation of the book.

The book covers several things that are happening right now.

AI: Mehlhorn doesn’t treat AI as a single superintelligence that wakes up and takes over. Instead, it shows up everywhere at once: in governance, in religion, in what you see through your glasses, and in who gets to decide what is true. Zhao’s algorithmic state and Kaspar’s attempts to rebuild shared truth are two different results that emerge from the same starting point.

Geopolitical chaos and global reorganization: The book assumes the post-World War II order is coming apart and is all about what fills the gap. Russia, China, the UK, the US, Venezuela, Kenya, Syria, and Jordan are the primary settings for the new structures that emerge, none of which map cleanly onto existing nation-states (well, except maybe China).

Economic extremes: The protagonists come from Silicon Valley, the UK, China, refugee camps, and a trafficking ring. Some of the most powerful systems get built by the people with the least resources, starting from nothing more than a tablet in a camp or an empire built from the bottom of the economy by an abused teenager who has the gift of synesthesia.

These things feed each other throughout the book over several decades. AI accelerates the geopolitical breakdown, the breakdown widens the economic gaps, and the people on the wrong side of those gaps use AI to build something of their own because society has left them behind.

While the beginning of the book goes back a few decades to create the origin stories of the characters, it quickly gets to today and then runs through 2039. That’s close enough that it’s near-term sci-fi, which is my favorite flavor.

In the foreword, Reid states that free societies must “evolve to survive the speed of autocracy without becoming autocratic themselves.” The book explores different approaches, including some with devastating moral costs.

Rob and Dmitri, congrats on a great first book.
